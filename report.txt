1. Abstract

This report proposes the Autonomous Container Search Assistant (ACSA): an AI intelligence layer to be deployed above an existing Terminal Operating System (TOS), which would advise on optimal container retrieval planning. The proposed architecture would read relevant container, equipment, and yard data from an existing TOS (such as Navis N4, Tideworks, or another commercial system), apply a mix of deterministic optimization and LLM-based orchestration to choose candidate container retrieval records, equipment, and route options, and recommend these to a human operator for approval prior to execution by the TOS.

This report concerns a proposed prototype and target architecture - not a system already deployed and validated in production by a terminal. As such, it takes the form of an engineering design document and startup whitepaper: the described architecture, technology choices, and roadmap concern what the prototype should build and demonstrate, beginning with a limited pilot at a single terminal zone before claiming terminal-wide or multi-port readiness. The performance targets (retrieval time, equipment use) named in this document are engineering goals to be validated during a pilot, not already achieved results.


2. Introduction

A terminal handling over 100,000 containers per year will have its cargo stored in stacks within yard blocks and moved between quay, yard, and gate by crane and truck. Most such terminals of this size already have a commercial TOS (Terminal Operating System) deployed: this is the authoritative system of record for container positions, equipment status, and yard and stowage planning. What such a system does not natively address is dynamic optimization of traffic and retrieval priorities based on current congestion and the relative value of different container retrieval goals. These are still mostly addressed by human yard planners, who have to make often time-critical judgements as to which crane or truck should be sent where, even if the TOS has up-to-date information on each individual container's location, equipment status, and yard storage.

The Autonomous Container Search Assistant proposes to take a different approach: to read a (primarily) read-mostly cache of container and equipment data from an existing TOS, enrich it with IoT checkpoint and event-stream data, and apply a set of cooperating software agents to recommend one or more retrieval options (route + equipment) for a given request. The value proposition is not one of merely another information repository (as the TOS already serves that role) but one of cooperative, incremental planning assistance with existing enterprise systems.


3. Problem Statement

For a terminal with a substantial volume of containers, a TOS alone does not address four interrelated issues which contribute to delays, equipment unutilization, and increased costs during retrieval.

Even if a TOS has accurate records on individual container positions, existing industry practices have problems with

3.1 Manual coordination: radio coordination of yard planners, crane operators, and truck drivers can result in equipment being driven or cranes being raised unnecessarily during shift transitions, with no shared view of what has already been retrieved.

3.2 Equipment allocation: crane and truck resources are often allocated manually by yard planners, using primarily static rosters and heuristics, with no automatic prioritization based on distance or value.

3.3 Retrieval latency: retrieving a non-top-of-stack container requires non-trivial repositioning even if the TOS has accurate records of its location.

3.4 Congestion: traffic lanes and crane positions can become unexpectedly congested during peak vessel arrival or departure periods, without adequate predictive modeling to reroute traffic.

These issues are problems of coordination and planning, not of information availability: this is why they are not solved by simply having richer information in the TOS. Rather, this is why the value proposition for the recommended solution concerns adding a layer of cooperative, recommendation-based planning intelligence above the TOS, rather than within it.


4. Objectives

Primary objective: design an AI-assisted recommendation layer that would read information from an existing TOS and other sources to propose container retrieval plans (route + equipment) for human operator approval, reducing planning overhead while retaining execution responsibility with the port operator.


4.1 Functional


Accept a retrieval request and locate the requested container with a local cache of TOS data, with fallback to direct querying of the TOS on cache miss


Recommend a retrieval plan (route + equipment) using a mix of optimization algorithms, cross-checking against the digital twin's known state for conflicts


Present the recommendation to a human operator, with configurable automation policies for routine, high-confidence requests and manual overrides


Track the status of a dispatched retrieval from initiation through confirmation, updating relevant dashboards accordingly


4.2 Technical


Sub-second lookup performance from the local cache, with TOS-level queries expected to be significantly slower but used only as a fallback for cache misses at scale


Every plan is checked against the digital twin's last-known state as a conflict check before being recommended to a human operator for approval


Backend services and the agentic AI layer are developed with scalability in mind, with no monolithic components


4.3 Business


Demonstrate, in a single-terminal pilot, reduced planning time per retrieval compared to a manual process before automation, without requiring the terminal to move away from its existing TOS


Collect a dataset of recommendation records for future analysis as a differentiating factor and long-term asset



5. System Architecture

The proposed architecture is organized in layers from human users and client applications through the API, backend services (with a synced cache of TOS data), agentic AI, deterministic optimization engines, event streaming, data storage, the digital twin, and IoT ingestion with monitoring across layers. It is deliberately decoupled to allow independent scaling of user interfaces, business logic, autonomous reasoning, and digital twinning of physical assets.

Figure 5.1 – Proposed system architecture and data flow, numbered per request/response flow from a user initiating a request (1) through task execution and status update (13). The existing TOS is presented as an external system which the Core Backend Services layer has read access to.

The numbered items describe a request flowing through the Load Balancer, Gateway, and Authentication (1-3) to the Agent Orchestrator, which in turn directs it to the Search Agent (4-5); the Decision Agent evaluates congestion forecasts and search results (6) and then directs route and equipment allocation to the Deterministic Optimization Engines via the Route and Equipment agents (7-8); the plan is validated against the Digital Twin and, with a recommendation, is then sent to the Core Backend Services layer (9); the Event Bus then notifies the worker's mobile application which acknowledges the task (10-11); finally, the completion is registered, and the status is updated for all dashboards (12-13). The step of supervisor approval referenced in section 8 falls between items 9 and 10 and is detailed in figures 13.1-13.2.

6. TOS Integration

A terminal operating at the scale of 100,000+ containers will almost certainly be using a commercial TOS (Navis N4, Tideworks, or another proprietary platform) for berth scheduling, yard/stowage planning, and equipment records. It would be commercially irrational for such a terminal to adopt ACSA as a replacement for their existing system of record (SOR). The proposed architecture deliberately leaves ACSA as an intelligence layer that is integrated with the TOS rather than the other way around.


6.1 Architecture Overview

The TOS Integration Adapter is the only component of the proposed architecture that interacts with the legacy TOS. It is designed to be largely read-only – it will periodically sync container records, equipment status, and yard/stowage planning data from whichever APIs or database views are available from the existing TOS (REST endpoints, EDI feeds, or direct database queries depending on the compatibility options offered by a specific vendor). It will also consume event streams from the TOS if they are available (gate moves, crane moves). The adapter will only ever write a recommendation to the TOS – it will not attempt to write to the TOS's database directly. Physical moves of equipment or changes to container status will be recorded in the TOS as they always have been by virtue of occurring at the terminal.

Figure 6.1 – Proposed integration architecture: the adapter reads information from the existing TOS (left) and writes recommendations back to it (right); actual execution of moves is captured in the TOS via the terminal's existing audit trails


6.2 Commercial Implications


No forklift upgrade necessary – a terminal can pilot the solution with a read-only or limited-write ETL process from their existing TOS with no changes to gate/crane operations


Reduced risk of adoption – the terminal's SOR and audit trails remain untouched; ACSA is an enhancement layer


Vendor neutrality – the adapter pattern allows for compatibility with multiple TOS platforms (Navis N4, Tideworks, etc) rather than building proprietary tooling for a single vendor

8. Agentic AI Layer

A crucial design decision about the proposed agent-based architecture is that it will handle orchestration, disambiguation, and explanation, whereas numerical computation (pathfinding, scheduling, forecasting) will be done by pluggable deterministic modules. In other words, the agent will decide what to compute, not how to compute it. An LLM-based planner is not used for route computation, equipment allocation, or congestion forecasting, since these have well-established deterministic algorithms. When a human-comprehensible plan needs to be generated for the workers, it is synthesized by the agent based on these computations. The LLM-based agent is used to choose which options to pursue, to request additional information or human approval when the confidence is low, and to explain the plan.

9. Data Management

The existing TOS continues to be the source of truth for master data on containers and equipment. Within the AGENT system, PostgreSQL acts as a cache (with periodic refreshes from the TOS and on relevant TOS events) for TOS data and holds ACSA's own data: retrieval tasks, route plans, and recommendation history. This is intentional, so as to not turn ACSA into another source of truth for the TOS data that already exists, while still allowing for ACSA to have up-to-date copies of the information for fast analytical or agentic operations. Redis is used for fast (sub-millisecond) lookups at scale and acts as the working memory for the agentic layer. A timeseries database is used to store telemetry and congestion data from IoT checkpoints. Blob storage is used for images, documents, or twin snapshots, at a low cost per gigabyte.

Figure 9.1 – Core operational schema. The container and Equipment tables are explicitly caches; the TOS is the source of truth for those.

Local cache reconciliation with TOS data is done on-demand for any queries involving data with a non-static value (the local copy of the data is always overwritten by the latest TOS values), while data with a static value is not reconciled. This presents a simple eventual consistency model, which does not endeavor to implement more complicated consensus algorithms for the relatively smaller scale of this application.

11. Event Bus

Kafka is proposed as the message bus which would decouple all producers (backend services, IoT gateway, agents, TOS Integration Adapter) from all consumers (Notification, Analytics, Monitoring), while also avoiding situations where slow consumers block faster producers, for example, in the case of task publishing. A web socket server is deployed to bridge the event bus with browser/mobile clients.

12. Digital Twin

The concept of a "digital twin" in this design is limited to a reconciled view of the yard state – the positions of containers (as per TOS data reconciled with RFID checkpoint data), equipment state, and a static (but informative) overlay of the yard's road/lane topology with dynamically updated congestion data from sensors. This is not a high-resolution recreation of the yard state and physics, as the fidelity of the position of any particular container is only as high as the TOS data or the last RFID checkpoint. The digital twin is used for validating plans (e.g., checking if there are any conflicting tasks, such as a route through a closed lane or simultaneous use of the same equipment) before actually attempting to realize them in the yard. Before accepting any plan, the agent responsible for it performs a feasibility check using the current digital-twin state.

Figure 12.1 – Digital twin synchronization. The state of the TOS and checkpoint events are reconciled into the best-known yard position, used to validate a plan before attempting to commit it.


13. AI Optimization Engines

These are the deterministic and statistical algorithms used by the agents, which constitute the core computational logic of the system. Route optimization uses a search algorithm to find the most efficient path between points; equipment allocation uses a generic optimization engine to find the best assignment of equipment to tasks given their constraints and preferences; congestion prediction is a statistical time-series algorithm. Algorithms have been selected wherever a well-established method (compared to an LLM) exists and is applicable (see the note in 13.5).

14. Security and Access Control

The proposed authentication design issues short-lived JWTs with role and scope claims after credentials validation, with multi-factor authentication required for Supervisor and Admin roles due to their ability to approve or override potential recommendations. All requests are subjected to role-based access control prior to being serviced by a scoped service, with all access decisions, both permitted and denied, logged to an immutable audit log. The TOS Integration Adapter is designed to have a narrow scope of access since it is the only component with access to the existing TOS, and thus it employs a separate service credential set to limit exposure.

Figure 14.1 – Authentication and authorization flow, including the audit logging path.


15. Monitoring and Observability

Backend services and agents are designed to expose logs and metrics to a central observability pipeline capturing both generic infrastructure metrics (latency, throughput, and error rate) and domain-specific agent metrics that would not be available in an off-the-shelf system: plan confidence score, rate of supervisor override, and tool-call success rate. We propose a Monitoring Agent specifically tasked with observing for reasoning loop conditions and reduced plan confidence trends and escalating to a human supervisor to break the loop rather than persisting with failed reasoning. The agent's logic is necessarily dependent on operational context and is best tuned on the pilot data.

17. Commercial Positioning


17.1 Why a Terminal Would Want This

The commercial argument is centered around the integration scenario described in section 6 – a terminal does not need to adopt a wholly new TOS, retrain its workforce on an entirely new system of record, or undertake the business risk of migrating its container and equipment data into a new database. Our intention is for ACSA to be installed as an add-on recommendation layer on top of whatever TOS the terminal already uses, with the option for a terminal to adopt our recommendation layer on a limited scope (e.g., per yard zone) with the existing TOS remaining in use in other areas. It reduces the commercial and operational risk compared to a solution proposing a wholesale replacement of a terminal's IT systems.


17.2 Cost and Time Value


Reduced planning effort: automating the candidate generation-ranking process reduces the time a planner must spend on iterative candidate ranking, letting them focus on exceptional cases with higher planning priority.


Reduced equipment idle time: congestion-aware route optimization reduces unnecessary equipment movement and waiting time compared to standard planner heuristics.


Reduced training time for new planners: a recommendation system that surfaces ranked, justified suggestions reduces the experience requirement for effective retrieval planning compared to relying on intuition alone


17.3 Differentiation from the Competition

Commercial TOS software such as Navis N4 and Tideworks are expected to continue to serve as a port's system of record – we do not intend to position ACSA as a direct competitor to their primary offerings. The differentiation from the competition is narrower: while ACSA is a general-purpose autonomous agent framework, it is being proposed as a narrow-use recommendation layer for the retrieval-planning judgment task, a role no TOS vendor has committed to filling. This has the advantage of being much more focused on a specific problem and reducing the sales challenge to convincing a terminal operator that ACSA is a viable replacement for their entire planning system of record. Instead, the commercial proposition is positioned as an enhancement to the existing system, which should be considerably easier to sell, particularly for an early-stage company without an established enterprise sales force.

19.2 Limitations


The accuracy of the digital-twin model depends on the frequency of TOS synchronization and the number of IoT checkpoints; early in deployment, few checkpoints means reduced confidence in the value of the pilot.


The orchestration logic based on LLMs may sometimes fail to correctly interpret ambiguous input, or make a recommendation with lower confidence than the piloters expect; thus, every recommendation must be routed through a confidence/policy gate before execution.


Reinforcement learning or other adaptive algorithms are not part of the initial prototype and would require substantial data from the pilot before they could improve upon the deterministic rules used at launch.

20. Conclusion and Future Scope

This document has outlined one possible technical approach to the problems described, rather than a specific product: a generic layer of AI intelligence running on top of whichever TOS a terminal chooses to use, implementing critical path decisions as deterministic algorithms and using LLMs for orchestration, disambiguation, and explanation, with all recommendations subject to human oversight. By focusing on an architecture that supplements rather than replaces an existing TOS, this design preserves commercial viability (as a pilot program for a terminal operator has limited risk and quick feedback) while being technically realistic (fewer features to implement and test).


20.1 Future Scope


Reinforcement learning for congestion/scheduling, once the pilot has produced enough data to train such a model


Vision-based container identification and damage assessment as a complement to RFID/QR codes

More extensive write-back capabilities to TOS, if and when a terminal is willing to engage in deeper integration (initial release will be read-only, as per section 17)


An operations copilot built into the LLM for natural language queries about yard state and recommendations, building on the explainability features in the Decision Agent
